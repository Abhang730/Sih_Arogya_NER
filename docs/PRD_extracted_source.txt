### [Heading2] 1.1 Source basis
This master document consolidates the project decisions in the Arogya-NER Master Context and the subsequent complete technical specification, plus the decisions refined during project discussions: whole-body-first screening, joint-risk localization, targeted second-stage assessment, modular wearable placement, protected patient identity, and offline/online model switching.

### [Heading2] 1.2 Binding product guardrails
Never describe the system as independently diagnosing osteoarthritis. Use screening, risk indication, further assessment, and referral-support terminology.
Never invent NER-specific clinical or genetic evidence. Regional hypotheses must be framed as hypotheses until locally validated.
Never quote unmeasured accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, or clinical performance. Use target metrics and validation status.
Clearly label what is real, prototype, simulated, planned, and clinically validated.
Use pose/sensor models for perception. A small language model, where used, operates on structured outputs rather than raw video.
Raw video should not be retained by default; retain derived landmarks/features necessary for research only when consent and governance permit.
Kellgren-Lawrence grading should not be represented as a clinical grade unless supported by appropriate radiographic labels and validation.
If Aadhaar is collected, protect it as a sensitive identifier and prefer an application-specific patient ID for all routine workflows and reports.

### [Heading1] 2. Executive Summary
Arogya-NER is an offline-first, mobile healthcare screening platform designed to extend basic musculoskeletal/OA risk screening into low-resource settings in the North Eastern Region. The product is deliberately built as a two-stage system rather than a single black-box model.

### [Heading2] 2.1 Product thesis

[TABLE]
STAGE 1 — WHERE IS THE CONCERN?Perform a short standardized whole-body assessment using the smartphone camera. Extract 33 body landmarks, analyze gait, posture and movement over time, and run a quantized TFLite model locally to generate joint-level potential risk-marker scores.
[/TABLE]

[TABLE]
STAGE 2 — WHAT DO WE KNOW ABOUT THAT JOINT?For a flagged joint, the healthcare worker launches a joint-specific protocol. The app combines a validated joint-specific questionnaire, targeted camera measurements, a small reusable wearable sensor pod, and optionally existing medical imaging/clinical reports. A multimodal model produces an explainable joint-level screening/referral indication.
[/TABLE]

### [Heading2] 2.2 Why this architecture
It directly covers the SIH request for joint movement analysis, gait/posture assessment, pain/mobility screening, portable sensor assessment, AI/ML risk analysis, digital records, multilingual workflows, offline collection, preventive guidance and specialist dashboards.
It avoids attaching hardware to every body region for every patient. The wearable is deployed only after Stage 1 identifies an area requiring closer assessment.
It creates a scalable platform: the camera/edge infrastructure is common, while each joint has a protocol, questionnaire, sensor-placement configuration and model.
It allows offline field triage while using stronger cloud models when connectivity returns, without making internet access a prerequisite for screening.
It is explainable by design: the clinical model uses XGBoost + SHAP; the final decision uses structured evidence rather than a single opaque end-to-end prediction.

### [Heading2] 2.3 MVP statement
For SIH, the first fully implemented joint module should be the knee because the existing project work already covers WOMAC, knee flexion/ROM, gait, sit-to-stand and the knee wearable configuration. The platform, however, should be engineered from the beginning as a multi-joint framework so that hip, shoulder, ankle, elbow and wrist protocols can be added without redesigning the entire system.

### [Heading1] 4. Problem, Users and Objectives

### [Heading2] 4.1 Problem statement in product terms
Remote and rural NER healthcare settings can lack specialist orthopaedic access and diagnostic infrastructure. The product opportunity is not to replace diagnostic imaging or specialists, but to provide a standardized first-line movement/symptom screening mechanism that helps health workers document patients, recognize potential movement-related risk markers, prioritize further assessment and referral, and preserve information for later specialist review.

### [Heading2] 4.2 Primary users

[TABLE]

ASHA worker | Fast, guided, low-literacy screening; offline; minimal typing; clear referral action | Android phone
ANM / healthcare worker | Repeatable protocol; patient records; sensor-assisted assessment | Android phone + Motion Pod
PHC supervisor | Screening volume, referral queue, worker performance, data quality | Web dashboard
Orthopaedic specialist | Patient history, questionnaire, movement features, reports, attachments, trends | Web dashboard
Patient | Understand why further assessment is advised; receive simple preventive guidance | Worker-assisted mobile / print/PDF report
[/TABLE]

### [Heading2] 4.3 Product goals
Provide a repeatable whole-body movement screen before targeted joint testing.
Localize potential joint-level movement risk markers from a short camera assessment.
Perform a joint-specific second-stage assessment only where indicated.
Combine subjective symptoms, biomechanics, wearable signals and available medical evidence in an explainable way.
Operate core screening and report generation without internet.
Create secure longitudinal digital records linked to the worker and healthcare location.
Support multiple NER languages and voice-assisted interaction over time.
Create a modular technical base for additional joints without duplicating the entire platform.

### [Heading2] 6.1 UX rule
The system should never make the healthcare worker guess which test to perform next. Every stage is protocol-driven. Joint selection controls the subsequent questionnaire, camera instructions, sensor placement and report structure.

### [Heading2] 6.2 Whole-body result semantics
Stage 1 produces a map of potential movement-related risk markers. It is a triage mechanism for selecting what to assess next, not a whole-body OA diagnosis.

### [Heading2] 6.3 Joint result semantics
Stage 2 produces a joint-specific screening indication and referral priority. It can summarize evidence and functional indicators but should not claim a radiographic OA stage without validated imaging evidence.

### [Heading1] 9. Stage 1 — Whole-Body Movement Screening Protocol

### [Heading2] 9.1 Goal
The whole-body protocol answers: “Which body region(s) show potential movement-related markers that justify a more detailed assessment?” It should be standardized enough to make the camera model data comparable across workers and devices.

### [Heading2] 9.2 Test sequence

[TABLE]
Step | Activity | Primary outputs
1 | Static standing capture | Pose quality, body alignment proxies, trunk/pelvis/knee relationship
2 | Controlled sit-to-stand / return | Functional movement, symmetry, lower-limb mechanics
3 | Short standardized walking sequence | Gait timing, symmetry, joint trajectories, trunk compensation
4 | Optional simple movement sequence | Broader range-of-motion / coordination signals where protocol permits
5 | Quality review | Pose confidence, framing, lighting and protocol adherence
[/TABLE]

### [Heading2] 9.3 Camera setup
Use a stationary smartphone camera whenever possible.
Guide the worker with an on-screen framing overlay.
Require the full body to be visible for the whole-body protocol.
Use camera/device orientation guidance and quality checks before recording.
If pose confidence is too low, ask the worker to reposition rather than silently accepting poor data.

### [Heading1] 10. AI Architecture — Stage 1 Whole-Body Risk Localization

### [Heading2] 10.1 Perception backbone
Primary candidate: MediaPipe BlazePose running on-device. The project also keeps YOLOv8n-pose as a benchmark/alternative where required. The selected pose engine outputs the body landmarks used by the temporal model.

### [Heading2] 10.2 Landmark representation
Video frames → 33 body landmarks / frame → normalized temporal sequenceExample joint chain:Hip → Knee → AnkleShoulder → Elbow → WristPelvis / trunk → lower-limb relationships

### [Heading2] 10.3 Temporal model
Primary architecture recommendation: a lightweight Spatial-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (ST-GCN). The skeleton is a graph and the sequence contains time, making ST-GCN a natural fit for learning relationships across connected body landmarks and movement over time.

### [Heading2] 10.4 Stage-1 model heads

[TABLE]
Head | Purpose
Gait head | Learn whole-body walking pattern representation
Posture head | Learn static/dynamic posture-related features
Movement head | Learn generalized movement quality / coordination features
Joint-score head | Produce per-joint potential risk-marker scores or screening-priority scores
Quality head | Estimate whether the measurement is sufficiently reliable for downstream use
[/TABLE]

### [Heading2] 10.5 Stage-1 output example
Right Knee       0.78  → Further assessment recommendedLeft Hip         0.46  → Further assessment recommendedRight Shoulder   0.61  → Further assessment recommendedOther areas      0.10–0.30  → No immediate targeted test from Stage 1
The numerical values above are illustrative UI examples, not validated thresholds.

### [Heading2] 10.6 Deployment
The whole-body model is exported to TensorFlow Lite and quantized for mobile deployment. INT8 quantization is the target production profile where accuracy and operator/device benchmarks show acceptable degradation.

### [Heading2] 11.2 Selection logic
Stage 1 ranks candidate joints by risk-marker score and measurement confidence.
The healthcare worker reviews the body map and selects one or more areas for detailed assessment.
The app loads the selected joint protocol and laterality.
The worker may defer a flagged joint if the patient declines or the protocol is not suitable; the reason is recorded.
If no joint exceeds the deployment-specific follow-up threshold, the app completes a low-intensity screening report and preventive guidance path.

### [Heading2] 11.3 Why human confirmation remains in the loop
The system is a screening assistant, not an autonomous diagnosis engine. A healthcare worker confirms which flagged joint to assess further, ensuring patient-reported symptoms, obvious injury, context and practical feasibility are considered before attaching hardware or conducting a targeted test.

### [Heading2] 13.4 Varus/valgus caution
Frontal-plane alignment and varus-thrust signals can be included as secondary research features, but monocular 2D estimation is inherently more sensitive to camera placement, clothing and occlusion. They should not dominate the primary risk model until benchmarked and validated.

### [Heading2] 14.4 Sampling and signal target
The current project specification targets BLE streaming at 50 Hz. Feature engineering and final sampling requirements must be confirmed experimentally for each protocol. The wearable should expose raw accelerometer and gyroscope channels plus device quality metadata.

### [Heading1] 15. Wearable Signal Processing and Sensor Quality

### [Heading2] 15.1 Core features
Acceleration magnitude / Euclidean norm for orientation-robust activity intensity representation
Angular velocity features
Segment-relative motion when dual IMUs are used
Movement duration and peak values
Gait-cycle timing proxies
Signal quality / dropouts / saturation indicators

### [Heading2] 15.2 Rotation robustness
Acc_magnitude = sqrt(Acc_x^2 + Acc_y^2 + Acc_z^2)
Euclidean acceleration magnitude can reduce dependence on sensor orientation for selected features, but it does not make the complete biomechanical signal “100% immune” to placement errors. Directional features and joint-angle estimates still depend on correct attachment and calibration.

### [Heading2] 15.3 Crepitus / vibration feature
High-frequency vibration variance is retained as an exploratory auxiliary signal rather than a core OA decision feature. The IMU is not, by itself, a validated crepitus diagnostic instrument. Any crepitus-related model must be separately benchmarked against clinical reference data.

### [Heading2] 15.4 Sensor quality engine
Camera quality      ─┐IMU quality          ─┼→ modality confidenceProtocol adherence   ─┤Data completeness    ─┘                         ↓                Confidence-aware fusion

### [Heading2] 15.5 Graceful degradation
If camera confidence is low, the system may rely more heavily on validated wearable/clinical features. If IMU quality is poor, the system can fall back to camera + clinical evidence. The exact fusion behavior should be learned/calibrated from validation data rather than fixed at arbitrary percentages.

### [Heading1] 17. AI Architecture — Stage 2 Joint-Specific Multimodal Model

### [Heading2] 17.1 Branch A — Camera temporal model
Use a lightweight temporal model such as a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) or 1D-CNN over joint-specific pose-derived features. For the knee, the main sequence is based on relevant landmarks and derived angle/trajectory features rather than the entire raw frame.

### [Heading2] 17.2 Branch B — Wearable temporal model
Use a compact 1D-CNN/TCN over accelerometer and gyroscope sequences. When dual IMUs are used, combine proximal and distal signals and derive relative motion features before the temporal encoder.

### [Heading2] 17.3 Branch C — Clinical model
Primary tabular model: XGBoost. Candidate benchmark: LightGBM. Explanation: SHAP feature contributions. Inputs include the selected joint questionnaire, general patient context and only those NER-specific exposure variables that are validated for the relevant task.

### [Heading2] 17.4 Optional Branch D — Imaging
When a validated imaging model exists for the target modality and joint, it becomes an optional fourth branch. Otherwise, imaging remains a specialist attachment rather than a numeric input to fusion.

### [Heading2] 17.5 Fusion
Initial fusion model: calibrated logistic regression. Future option: small MLP when data volume supports it. Inputs are branch scores/features plus modality confidence/quality signals.
Camera temporal score ────────┐IMU temporal score ───────────┤Clinical XGBoost score ───────┼→ Calibrated fusion → joint resultOptional imaging evidence ────┤Modality quality/confidence ──┘

### [Heading2] 21.2 Local storage
Recommended: SQLite protected with SQLCipher or an equivalent platform-approved database encryption layer. Data should be encrypted at rest and keys should be protected using platform secure storage. Local data should be minimized, and raw video should not be retained by default.

### [Heading1] 26. AI Data Strategy and Validation

### [Heading2] 26.1 Development datasets
Initial model development may use public OA/movement datasets identified for the relevant tasks, including OAI, MOST and appropriate public knee-OA datasets, but these datasets are not a substitute for NER-specific validation.

### [Heading2] 26.2 NER validation dataset
Participant  ├── Demographics / context  ├── Whole-body motion sequences  ├── Joint-specific motion sequences  ├── Wearable data (where applicable)  ├── Questionnaire responses  ├── Clinical assessment / specialist label  └── Imaging where clinically available                 ↓        TRAIN / VALIDATE / TEST

### [Heading2] 26.3 Label strategy
The exact target label for each module must be clinically defined before training. Labels may be specialist assessment categories, validated screening outcomes, functional severity categories, or radiographic reference grades when appropriate. The platform should not force a KL-grade prediction target simply because an imaging dataset is available.

### [Heading1] 27. ML Engineering Lifecycle
DATA INGESTION      ↓QUALITY FILTERING      ↓ANNOTATION / CLINICAL LABELING      ↓TRAIN / VALIDATE / TEST SPLIT      ↓BASELINE MODELS      ↓MODEL COMPARISON      ↓CALIBRATION      ↓QUANTIZATION      ↓ON-DEVICE BENCHMARK      ↓PILOT VALIDATION      ↓MODEL RELEASE WITH VERSION + METRICS

### [Heading2] 27.1 Recommended model progression
Stage 1 baseline: engineered features + simple classifier to establish a benchmark before ST-GCN.
Stage 1 production candidate: lightweight ST-GCN with confidence head.
Stage 2 camera baseline: 1D-CNN/TCN over engineered movement features.
Stage 2 IMU baseline: engineered sensor features + shallow classifier.
Clinical model: XGBoost + SHAP.
Fusion baseline: calibrated logistic regression.
Only after enough data: compare small MLP fusion and deeper temporal models.
Export the approved edge models to TFLite INT8 and benchmark accuracy, latency, memory and battery impact.

### [Heading2] 27.2 Model versioning
Every AI result must store the model identifier, version, protocol version, device/app version and timestamp. A model update must not invalidate historical results or silently alter prior records.

### [Heading1] 33. Risks, Weaknesses and Mitigation

[TABLE]
Risk / weakness | Impact | Mitigation
No NER-specific labelled dataset | High | Treat public data as development data; run NER pilot/clinical validation before performance claims
Monocular varus/valgus estimation noisy | High | Make knee flexion/ROM/gait primary; require standardized frontal capture for secondary alignment features
Pose degradation in low light / clothing | High | Quality gate; device benchmark; confidence-aware modality fusion; field protocol
Wearable placement variation | Medium/High | Clear placement guide; calibration; quality score; dual-pod protocol where needed
Crepitus signal not clinically validated | Medium | Keep as research/auxiliary feature until validated
KL-grade temptation | High | Do not expose as clinical grade without radiographic labels and validation
Aadhaar sensitivity | High | Minimal collection; encryption/tokenization; use Arogya Patient ID operationally
Small dataset for deep models | High | Use lightweight models, strong baselines, transfer learning where appropriate, careful cross-validation
Cloud model differs from edge model | Medium | Model versioning; show edge vs cloud result separately
Over-referral | Medium | Calibrate thresholds and evaluate referral workload in pilot
Under-referral | High | Prioritize sensitivity for high-priority cases and monitor false negatives
Multilingual mistranslation | Medium | Native-speaker review, versioned clinical strings, TTS QA
Worker burden too high | Medium | Protocol-driven UI, short tests, minimal typing, optional wearable only after Stage 1
Competitive differentiation | Medium | Lead with NER-specific exposure context, whole-body localization + targeted multimodal workflow, offline explainability, not generic “AI diagnosis” claims

### [Heading1] 34. Product Strengths and Known Limitations

### [Heading2] 34.1 Strengths
Two-stage architecture minimizes unnecessary hardware use
Whole-body movement screening is aligned to the broader SIH wording around joint movement, gait and posture
Reusable sensor hardware supports multiple joints
Offline-first design directly fits remote deployment
Explainable ML stack rather than one black-box model
Clinical, biomechanical and contextual evidence are kept separate until fusion
Longitudinal digital records enable follow-up
Privacy-by-design avoids routine raw-video retention
Architecture can expand from knee to hip, shoulder, ankle, elbow and wrist

### [Heading2] 34.2 Known limitations
The whole-body AI must be trained and clinically validated to localize real joint risk markers
Stage-1 movement abnormality is not equivalent to OA
Sensor vibration is not a standalone OA diagnostic signal
A smartphone camera cannot replace imaging
Clinical thresholds and referral policies need local governance
Performance may vary across phones, clothing, lighting and operator technique
NER-specific model validity remains unproven until locally collected data is available
